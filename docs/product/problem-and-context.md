# Problem and context

The [final SRS](<ScrapTrace_Software_Requirements_Specification (2).docx>) governs application requirements and acceptance. This page preserves the evidence-backed problem context and citations from the [Concept Note](../../concept_note.md); it does not add new factual claims.

## Context

Electronic waste includes discarded refrigerators, televisions, computers, air conditioners, and other electrical items. The Concept Note cites the *Global E-waste Monitor 2024*: 62 million tonnes were generated globally in 2022 and 22.3% was formally recorded as collected and recycled. In Ghana, collectors, buyers, repairers, and dismantlers recover valuable materials, but much activity is not linked through formal records.

## Missing collection records

A final received weight may not reveal who collected an item, where and when it was found, its condition, how it moved, or whether the same photograph or load was reported twice. Without linked collector and recycler records, programmes have weaker evidence for planning, incentives, and discrepancy review.

## Limited access

The Concept Note reports that suitable formal collection points and advanced recycling facilities can be difficult to find in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa. A household may not know where to take an old appliance; a collector may know only an informal buyer. Programmes consequently have weaker signals about where collection points, transport, or capacity are needed.

## Health and handling risks

Burning cables and plastics can release toxic smoke. Breaking, heating, or cutting items can expose people to substances including lead, mercury, and cadmium. The Concept Note highlights risks to lungs, the brain, and the nervous system, especially for children, pregnant people, and nearby families. People need simple, category-relevant guidance that avoids encouraging hazardous dismantling.

## Economic pressure

Breaking or burning an item may offer rapid access to valuable material, while safer handling can require transport, equipment, and care. When safe delivery has no clear benefit, harmful shortcuts may remain economically attractive. The Concept Note therefore connects trustworthy delivery evidence with the possibility of a separately authorised safe-delivery incentive; it does not promise one.

## Weak programme evidence

Reports of aggregate recycled weight may lack a clear trail from collection through receipt and processing. This limits the ability to detect duplicates, investigate mismatches, understand local collection patterns, or compare collection claims with recycler receipts. No single photograph, location, or QR code is sufficient proof.

## Opportunity

Linking in-app capture, approved safety guidance, a suitable participating destination, collection records, recycler-confirmed weight, processing evidence, and human review can make the journey more actionable and auditable. The Concept Note grounds this direction in a GIZ Accra pilot that recorded 27.5 tonnes through 1,389 transactions involving an incentive for unburned cable delivery. ScrapTrace proposes applying the collection-and-evidence principle across more e-waste categories; equivalent outcomes are not yet demonstrated.

## Legal context

The Concept Note references Ghana's Hazardous and Electronic Waste Control and Management Act, 2016 (Act 917) as the national legal framework and notes that the national e-waste fund may support collection and safe recycling. ScrapTrace documentation is not legal advice, and programme rules, regulatory compliance, recycler status, incentives, and reporting acceptance require competent-authority confirmation.
